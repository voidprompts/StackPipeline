We ran Clay alongside three competing enrichment tools for 90 days, pushing an identical
5,000-contact list through each and manually verifying a 200-record sample from every
provider. This review covers what we measured, not what the vendor claims.

<Callout variant="choice" title="Clay is our top pick for enrichment at scale">
If you enrich more than 2,000 contacts a month, Clay's waterfall approach costs less than
the point solutions it replaces while returning materially better data. Below that volume,
a single provider is the more rational purchase.
</Callout>

## How waterfall enrichment actually works

Traditional enrichment queries one vendor. If that vendor has no record, the field stays
empty and you have still paid for the lookup. Clay queries providers in a sequence you
define, stopping at the first one that returns a result — and only charges for the
successful call.

That difference compounds. On our test list, no single provider exceeded a 78% email
match rate. Clay's waterfall, querying seven providers in sequence, reached 91%.

<Callout variant="warn" title="Credit forecasting is the real learning curve">
Clay's pricing is not per contact — it is per enrichment call, and a single row can consume
anywhere from 0.5 to 8 credits depending on how many waterfall steps run. Budget a two-week
calibration period before committing to an annual plan.
</Callout>

## Where Clay falls short

European coverage is the clearest gap. Our EU segment matched at 82% versus 96% for US
contacts — still the best result in our test, but a meaningful drop if your market is
primarily European.

Table performance is the second issue. Past roughly 50,000 rows the interface becomes
sluggish, and we saw multi-second delays on column operations. Clay is not a data
warehouse, and trying to use it as one will frustrate you.

## Who should buy Clay

Buy it if enrichment volume is above 2,000 records monthly, you currently pay for multiple
data providers, or your match rates are actively limiting pipeline. Skip it if you enrich
a few hundred records a month — the $149 floor will not pay for itself.
